# 18th-Century Biography: Melancholy, Hypochondria, and Religion

## Scope

Use this note when reviewing eighteenth-century biography passages about
melancholy, hypochondria, madness, nervous disorders, religious conscience, or
devotional reading.

## Defect Families

- Historical medical terms should not be flattened into modern diagnoses.
  `hypochondria` and `hypochondriack` can refer to a broad nervous or melancholy
  condition, not only modern illness anxiety. Use context-rich Chinese such as
  "疑病性忧郁" or "疑病性忧郁患者" when the passage treats the historical syndrome.
- Keep Boswell's distinction between disordered imagination and impaired
  judgement. Do not translate every `madness` occurrence with the same modern
  psychiatric label; preserve whether the source is fear, self-description,
  philosophical distinction, or actual judgement impairment.
- `vapours`, `low spirits`, and related melancholy terms need historical
  nervous-register wording. Avoid literal steam imagery for `vapours`.
- Religious indifference and devotional-book passages often hinge on the
  difference between talk, thought, practice, and rational inquiry. Preserve
  emphatic source contrasts such as `talker` versus `think`.

## Review Checklist

- Build a mini-watchlist for `melancholy`, `hypochondria`, `vapours`,
  `madness`, `imagination`, `judgement`, `spirits`, `religion`, `talk`, and
  `think`.
- Check whether the Chinese term names a historical condition, a personal fear,
  a philosophical distinction, or a devotional habit.
- Read the paragraph without the source. It should sound like historical
  biography, not a modern clinical case report or a vague mood summary.
- Any correction to a mental-state term or religious contrast makes that review
  round `FIXED_RECHECK_REQUIRED`; rerun the full-chapter pass before PASS.
