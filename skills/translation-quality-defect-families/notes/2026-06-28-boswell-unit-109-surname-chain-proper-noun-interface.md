# Boswell Unit 109: Surname-Chain Proper-Noun Interfaces

## Context

Eighteenth-century editorial notes often use compact surname chains such as `Edwards`, `Tonson the Bookseller`, `Hayman the Painter`, or `Mr. L.` because the note assumes a learned reader can identify the people and roles. In Chinese translation, a bare transliterated surname chain can become opaque, especially when the note's argument depends on distinguishing author, bookseller, painter, critic, and disguised identity.

## Defect Pattern

- Translating compact English surname chains into Chinese surnames only can hide the evidentiary relation between people.
- A first-use omission is especially risky when a later note identifies an abbreviated person, such as `Mr. L.` as Lyttelton.
- Overloading every name with source forms is also undesirable; the source interface should appear where identification or role matters.

## Prevention Checklist

- In bibliographical and anecdotal notes, mark first-use source forms for people whose role is part of the evidence chain.
- Preserve compact source abbreviations such as `Mr. L.` when the note is explaining the abbreviation, then give the resolved Chinese name plus source form.
- Do not add source parentheses to every repeated ordinary mention after the first natural body occurrence.
- Record the source-interface decision in the chapter control when a note turns on identifying a person from a surname or initial.

## Applied In Unit 109

- Added source interfaces for Edwards, Tonson, Hayman, Lyttelton, and Sir George Lyttelton.
- Left later repeated mentions in Chinese only, except where source forms such as `Mr. L.` were themselves evidence.
